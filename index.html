Context Engineering Series · Part 9

The Harness That
Improves Itself

Part 8 made the harness safe and portable. It could catch how work goes wrong, and a human held the key. But it still couldn't do one thing... write its own improvements. Part 9 closes that loop, and the only reason it's safe to close is a check the loop is not allowed to grade for itself.

propose → gate → promote or revert· a held-out that never feeds back· autonomy on a leash

What Part 8 Left Half-Built

Part 7 built a guard that could grade my work. Part 8 gave that guard a spine... it caught more than voice, it traveled across runtimes, and a human, not the agent, held the key that could change it. But everything in Part 8 still waited for me to decide what to change. The guard could say "this is wrong." It couldn't say "here's a better version, and here's the proof it's better."

That's the half Part 7 deferred out loud, when I said the self-writing part was maybe an October job. This is October. The reason it was worth waiting is that a loop which writes its own changes is only as safe as the thing that decides which changes to keep. Build that decider wrong and "autonomous" just means "autonomously gets worse in a way that looks like progress." So the whole of Part 9 is really about one component: the check.

The Loop

The shape is small. Pick a target the loop is allowed to touch. A cheap model proposes a change. Then the change has to clear two gates before anything happens: it can't make any existing capability worse, and it has to improve at least one thing we declared we cared about, by a margin we can actually measure. Whatever survives gets checked one more time against a set of cases it has never seen and cannot influence. Clear that, and the change is kept. Fail it, and the change is thrown away and the baseline stands.

Nothing here is exotic. A coding agent already proposes changes all day. The two gates are the same preserve-and-extend rule the voice work used from the start. The one piece that makes it autonomy instead of a suggestion box is that last check, the sealed one, and the discipline that it never, ever gets to teach the proposer how to pass it.

The cheater is the point. It sails through the two gates the proposer can see, because it was built to. The sealed held-out is the only thing that catches it, and it can only catch it because the proposer was never allowed to learn what's in it. Remove that wall and the loop optimizes the wall instead of the work.

Why the Held-Out Stays Sealed

Any metric a system is optimized against stops being a good metric. Point a loop at a score and it will find the cheapest way to move the number, which is rarely the same as doing the work well. The only defense that holds is to keep a set of cases the optimizer can't see and can't tune against, and to score against those last, and to let a failure there be final... no re-optimizing until the held-out finally says yes, because that's just teaching it the answers slowly.

I didn't invent that discipline, and I find it reassuring that I didn't. NVIDIA's SoL-Pi work builds harness improvements this way and names the sealed, no-feedback held-out as the thing that stops the loop from overfitting itself. Salesforce's own research, SFR-AutoR&D, does the strict version for code: the improved function has to produce byte-identical output to the original across a big matrix of cases before any speedup counts. That's an oracle you can't sweet-talk. When a competitor's lab and your own lab both land on "the check is the hard part," you stop treating it as a detail.

Autonomy Inside a Fence

Here's where custody from Part 8 does its second job. The loop is allowed to run free on things that don't matter if it gets one wrong... a log compressor, a formatting pass, throwaway targets. The moment a proposed change touches the safety layer itself, the checks, the trust root, the rules about who can approve what, it is not applied. It's handed to the human at the custody line, the same line the builder couldn't cross in Part 8.

So "fully autonomous" has a hard boundary baked in, and it's the honest kind. It never means the loop rewrites its own guardrails while no one's looking. It means the loop improves the work, and a person still owns the thing that decides what "improved" is allowed to mean.

What's Proven, and What's Still a Bet

Worth separating what ran from what I'm arguing. The pieces of the loop exist and work. There's a hand-built version of the whole shape... a compressor whose own verifier rejects it and falls back to the original if it can't prove the compression was faithful. And the loop ran for real on a governed agent I built for Part 10: the held-out check caught a genuine gap, I fixed it, re-ran, and it went green. Propose, gate, catch, fix, re-gate... that cycle is not a diagram, it happened.

What I am not claiming is compounding. One gated improvement is real. A flywheel where each improvement makes the next one easier is a hope, not a result... SoL-Pi calls that a future direction too, and I'll hold myself to the same honesty. One good change at a time, each earned against a check that can fail.

The same gate, one more job

The Loop Also Picks the Brain

The thing the loop proposes doesn't have to be an instruction. It can be a cheaper model. Most tasks don't need a frontier brain... mapping some fields, reformatting, a routine bit of code. So the loop can propose running a route on a smaller, cheaper model, and that proposal faces the exact same wall as any other change: the sealed held-out eval. Keep the cheaper brain only if it clears the bar it can't see, and fall back to the bigger one if it doesn't. A cheaper model is never assumed adequate, it's earned against a check that can fail.

There's a second reason to reach for a different model, and it has nothing to do with cost. When the thing being checked is my own work, the worst judge is another copy of me, because it shares my blind spots. So the check that decides what's kept runs on a different lab's model than the one that proposed the change. Proposer and judge are different brains on purpose. I measured this earlier in the series: a same-model judge graded its own house style about twelve points too kind. A different-vendor judge doesn't.

Both of these are wired and tested, and neither runs on its own yet. A cheaper model stays a candidate until an eval promotes it, and nothing is promoted today. That's the honest state, and it's the right order... the mechanism should exist before the autonomy does.

The Places I Don't Trust It Yet

The whole thing rests on one un-gameable check, and not every check is un-gameable. Code with a differential test is the good case, where the original function is a perfect oracle. A judge scoring an agent's answer is the weak case, because a clever loop can learn to please the judge. Where the oracle is soft, the sealed held-out is doing all the work, and I trust the loop exactly as far as I trust that wall.

Sealed is a discipline, not a lock. Nothing physically stops a careless operator from letting held-out results leak back into the proposer. The isolation is enforced by how the loop is wired, and a wiring mistake quietly turns autonomy back into overfitting. That failure is silent, which is the dangerous kind.

Every number here is measured on my own targets. The published results from other labs are theirs, on their harnesses and their benchmarks. I don't get to borrow their percentages, and I haven't. When I quote an improvement, it's one I measured on the thing in front of me.

The Part That Surprised Me

For eight parts the harness was something I improved by hand. Part 9 is the first time it improves itself, and what surprised me is that the hard, interesting engineering isn't the part that writes the change at all, it's the part that refuses to be fooled by it. The autonomy didn't come from a smarter proposer, it came from a check the proposer can't see or argue with, sitting behind a fence it can't climb.

Which leaves one question, and it's the one the whole series has been walking toward. If the durable thing is the harness, the checks and the custody and the loop, and none of it depends on which model you rent this quarter... then where should all of that actually live? Not in my scaffold. Part 10 puts it where it belongs: in the platform itself.
